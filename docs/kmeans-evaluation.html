Unusual purchases: K-means vs the 3x median rule

Data: a seeded sample user. Each category's real amounts count as normal; one unusual purchase at a time is planted per category, at 1.5x, 2x, 4x, 8x its highest amount. K-means uses log(amount), k by silhouette (2 to 4, under 0.75 means one habit), k-means++ with 5 restarts; under 10 expenses the app uses its simple rule instead.

Elbow: inertia by k

Inertia always falls as k grows; the bend is where more clusters stop helping. The app picks k by silhouette; Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz in the table are the other two standard checks of the same clustering, for comparison only.

Choosing k: the app's rule vs the gap statistic

The app picks k by the highest silhouette and calls it one habit (k = 1) below 0.75, since silhouette can't score k = 1. The gap statistic (Tibshirani, Walther & Hastie, 2001) is the textbook test that can: it compares the clustering with uniform random data. They agree on 4 of 5 categories. Where they differ, look at the amounts: exact repeats (fixed fares like ₱13, ₱15, ₱20) score high on silhouette as separate groups, while the gap statistic sees no more structure than random; and the gap statistic's uniform reference can read a skewed single habit as two groups.
